Archimedes Sound Fidelity

How the Archimedes gets sound out of VIDC, what the motherboard filter was for, and what we can change in QTM to make a filter-bypassed machine sound as good as it can.

Tags: datasheet stated by Acorn · model simulated, not yet measured · needs recording only the hardware can answer.

How VIDC makes sound

QTM does not mix channels into one stream. VIDC has up to 8 channel slots and a single DAC. QTM writes each MOD channel into the DMA buffer interleaved with the others (its "DMA gap" is the channel count), and VIDC plays the bytes one after another, each slot with its own stereo position. The channels are summed afterwards, in the analogue circuit, by averaging the slots.

  • datasheet A 7-bit exponential DAC plus a sign bit. The sign steers the current to a "+" or a "−" pair of pins, and the board integrates and subtracts the two.
  • datasheet 8 chords of 16 steps, each chord's step twice the last: an approximation to µ255 law. Levels run from 0 to 247i, the smallest step is i/16, so about 4 000:1, or roughly 72 dB.
  • datasheet Stereo is time division: each slot's active time is split between the left and right pins, with 7 positions. The first quarter of every slot is muted for DAC settling.
  • datasheet The sample rate register counts in 1 µs per byte, minimum 3 µs. RISC OS's 48 µs at 4 channels is 12 µs per slot, 3 µs of it muted.
  • datasheet The board's integrating time constant "must be long enough to integrate over a complete sample cycle". That integrator is part of the output circuit, so it is probably still there after the bypass.

VIDC1 DAC level by magnitude code

Units of i = Iref/8. Each chord of 16 codes doubles the step size. From the datasheet, p.33.

One consequence matters most. At loud levels the top chord's step is 1/32 of full scale, about 2 bits coarser than an 8-bit linear MOD sample. At quiet levels the log format is finer than 8-bit linear, but only if the source has the resolution to use it.

What the motherboard filter was for

The only figure in the stardot thread is that the filter "chops all frequencies above 10kHz". That is roughly half the default 20.8 kHz sample rate, so it is a reconstruction filter sized for the default rate. It removed the DAC images above about 10 kHz, the switching energy from the slots and stereo, and what IanJeffray calls "VIDC whine". The A4, A7000 and Risc PC don't have it. SKS1, who worked on early Arcs, fitted a better filter to an A310 prototype and it "sounded no better", which fits with the model results below: the worst distortion is inside the audio band, where no filter can reach.

What the model found

These come from tools/audio/analyse_model.py. It models the datasheet's DAC curve and QTM's actual tables and code: the linear-to-log table that setlinlogtab builds from Sound_SoundLog, the volume table from setvoltabs, and the SUBS / MOVMI volume step in a3_anyvolume. Test signals are sines, measured by SINAD (signal against everything else) and THD.

QTM's conversion table rounds up

model Compared with the datasheet curve, the RISC OS table maps a linear sample x to about 2.04x i. Where it doesn't land exactly on a level, it always picks the one above: in 56 of the 107 inputs from 4 to 110 it picks a higher level than the nearest, and never a lower one. Inputs 120 to 127 all share the top code.

Volume as a subtract on the log code

model QTM's volume is a subtract from the log byte, clamped at zero, which is cheap and mostly works. Three things show up:

  • Only 46 of the 65 MOD volumes are distinct. Volumes 60 to 64 all play at full level, and from 32 upwards they come in pairs.
  • Most volumes play 0.2 to 0.7 dB louder than they should, which is the same rounding-up again.
  • At volumes 1 and 2, clamping at zero cuts off the quiet parts of the wave: SINAD is 17.7 and 23.8 dB, against about 30 dB for the best a log DAC could do.

Volume error by MOD volume

QTM's actual gain minus the ideal gain, in dB. Above zero means louder than it should be.

Stepping through samples

model The test plays a looped sample with 5 sine cycles in 64 bytes, at C-3, C-4 and C-5, with the sample period at 48, 32 and 24 µs. The distortion in the audible band comes out at −17 dB whatever the sample period. Most of it is images of the sample's own rate, and Paula on the Amiga makes those too. A faster sample period doesn't reduce it. Storing the sample band-limited at 2× its rate brings it down to −23 dB at every rate tested, at no extra runtime cost (only twice the memory).

This test sample is deliberately bright. Lower-pitched content within a sample distorts less, by about 6 dB per octave.

  • Time-scaling. The digital side scales exactly with the sample period. A test at 48 µs with tones at half frequency shows the image pattern of 24 µs, below 24 kHz. Only the analogue path doesn't scale, and the frequency response test measures that separately.
  • The recorder's own filter. The UCA222 is thought to use TI's PCM2902; its filter hasn't been checked. A spur that only appears at 24 µs and moves when the tone changes is the recorder, not the Arc.
  • Level. Set the gain with a full-scale tone first. The bypass may also skip the headphone driver.
  • Ground loops. Record silence with the Arc on and idle. A laptop on battery rules out a loop.
  • Unlocked clocks. The scripts will estimate each tone's exact frequency rather than trust nominal rates.

Test 1 in detail

The program, build/codesweep,ff8, takes over the sound system the way QTM does. It hands Sound_Configure its own channel handler, runs 1 channel at 48 µs with stereo centre, and puts the old configuration back at the end or on Escape. Each step is a square against code 0 rather than +code against −code, because the coupling capacitors block DC: a symmetric square would only show the sum of the two halves, so a mismatch between them would be invisible.

The analyser finds the two markers and works out the Arc's real sample rate in the recorder's clock from their spacing. It then measures each step's 100 Hz fundamental over whole cycles and scales the levels to the datasheet's units using the upper chords. It reports the error per code in steps of its chord, the −/+ ratio per chord, the noise floor, the left/right balance and which byte layout fits. It also writes a 256-byte linear-to-log table built from the measured levels, in QTM's format.

Under Arculator, whose conversion table is the datasheet curve, the analysis recovers that curve, apart from a fixed 0.02 i at the lowest codes. That 0.02 i is integer rounding in Arculator's audio path, about 82 dB below full scale. It picks the RISC OS byte layout (residual 0.02 i, against 55 i for sign-in-D7).

How to run and record it: tools/audio/README.md.

Test 1 results: A3020, filter bypassed

Recorded on the UCA222 at line-in level 60, peaking at −3.9 dBFS with no clipping. Files: recordings/codesweep-test1-a3020-no-filter-level-60.*.

Repeated in MODE 12 (24 MHz clock, rate 20 833 Hz as nominal; recordings/codesweep-test1-mode12-a3020-no-filter-level-60.*). Chords 4–7 agree with the first run to within 0.02 steps and the low chords to within about 0.1 steps. The ± mismatch and the drops at codes 48 and 64 repeat to within 0.03 i, and the two measured tables differ in 6 of 256 entries, each by one code. The DAC's behaviour is stable, and the screen mode and clock don't change it.

The sound runs 4.9% fast

The marker came out at 1 093 Hz, not 1 041.7 Hz: the Arc played 21 855 bytes a second where RISC OS asked for 20 833. That works out to VIDC running from a 25.18 MHz clock instead of the 24 MHz RISC OS assumes. The machine was in MODE 27, 640×480 VGA, which uses the 25.175 MHz clock: on the A3020 the VIDC clock follows the screen mode, so the sound rate does too. Future recordings use MODE 12, which runs from 24 MHz. Uncorrected, music plays about 0.83 semitones sharp; QTM already has code to measure the clock (checkclockcalc). For these tests it only matters that the analyser measures the real rate, which it now does.

The board does reroute the bits

The RISC OS byte layout, with the sign in bit 0, fits with a residual of 2.5 i, against 55 i for the datasheet's sign-in-D7. The board does route the bits as RISC OS expects.

The negative half is 0.54 dB quieter

The difference is the same in every chord, from 0.53 to 0.57 dB, so it is a plain gain difference between the + and − output paths, not a difference in the curve. A wave's negative half comes out about 6% smaller than its positive half, which makes second-harmonic distortion: −37 dB at full scale with the OS table. A table that aims each half at its own measured levels cancels it.

The low chords' steps are too big, and the DAC is not monotonic

Scaled to the datasheet, chords 4 to 7 are within 1–4% of it. Chords 1 to 3 have steps 11–20% bigger than they should be, so the level climbs too fast within each chord and then falls back at the next. At codes 48 and 64 it falls below the code before: by 0.39 i and 0.35 i. Within a chord the error grows in jumps at the binary bits of the step number, about half a step at step 8, so the DAC's step currents are binary-weighted with mismatched weights. Both halves show the same pattern to within 0.05 steps, so the curve is common to both paths and only the gain differs.

Tests 2–5 results: A3020, filter bypassed

All in MODE 12 at line-in level 60, files in recordings/. The noise test could only show MODEs 12 and 13 on this monitor.

The bypass leaves a 3.2 kHz roll-off

The response falls steadily above 1 kHz: −3 dB at about 3.7 kHz relative to the flat region, −10 dB at 10 kHz and −16 dB at 20 kHz. It is the same at 24 µs and 48 µs, so it belongs to the analogue circuit, not the sample rate. A single resistor-capacitor low-pass at 3.2 kHz, a time constant of 49.5 µs, fits it to within 0.05 dB rms. That is the integrator the datasheet describes, whose time constant "must be long enough to integrate over a complete sample cycle", sized for the default 48 µs period. The bypass removes the filter after it, not the integrator itself. The bass is fine: the coupling capacitors only start to cut in at about 4 Hz.

Frequency response, A3020 with the filter bypassed

Level relative to 1 kHz, with the DAC's own pulse shape taken out, so this is the analogue path.

24 µs48 µsfirst-order low-pass at 3.2 kHz

What this means. This is now the largest audible flaw: treble is 10 dB down at 10 kHz even with the filter bypassed. Because it is a plain first-order roll-off, it can be undone exactly, by a treble boost after the Arc, by a smaller integrator capacitor on the board (which only makes sense with a sample period of 24 µs or less), or by boosting treble in the samples during the offline prep pass. That last option suits the log DAC well: its error is a fixed fraction of the level, so turning the samples down to make room for the boost costs little quality.

Where the roll-off comes from: the audio hybrid

The A3010/A3020/A4000 circuit diagram (Heron 'E' main PCB, drawing 0194,500 sheet 2) puts the audio on a thick-film module, 0194007 Audio Hybrid (IM2). The drawing shows the topology but not the values inside it. Each channel has two stages:

  1. A subtractor that also integrates. An LM324 stage takes VIDC's "+" and "−" pin currents, through load resistors to SndVDD, and subtracts them. Its feedback is a resistor and capacitor in parallel, which makes it a first-order low-pass: the 3.2 kHz pole measured above.
  2. The filter and driver. Three resistors in series, a capacitor to ground at the first and third junctions, and one from the output back to the middle: a third-order Sallen-Key low-pass on one LM324 stage. It is buffered by a BC239 emitter follower, then the output passes through C60/C68 (100 µF) and 3.3 Ω to the headphone socket. The internal speaker's LM386 takes left and right mixed through 4.7 kΩ.

The A3010/A3020/A4000 bypass from the stardot thread wires LM324 pins 7 and 8, the outputs of stage 1, to C60 and C68. That skips stage 2 and its transistor driver and keeps stage 1, which is exactly what test 2 shows: one clean pole at 3.2 kHz and nothing after it. It also means the headphone socket is now driven by the op-amp alone through 3.3 Ω: fine at line level, but low-impedance headphones may distort.

The ± mismatch from test 1 probably comes from the tolerances of stage 1's subtractor resistors rather than from VIDC. If it does, a second machine will show a different mismatch.

A3010 results: the original filter

Tests 1 to 3 on an A3010 with its filter in place, in MODE 12 at line-in level 60. Files: recordings/*-a3010-filter-level-60.*.

The original filter is a fourth-order low-pass at 2.8 kHz

The response is flat to 2 kHz, rising 0.2 dB at 1.6 kHz, then falls fast: −5 dB at 3.2 kHz, −18.5 dB at 5 kHz, −41 dB at 10 kHz and −64 dB at 20 kHz, 24 dB per octave at the top. Taking the integrator as the A3020's 3.2 kHz, the hybrid's third-order filter fits as a real pole at 4.25 kHz and a resonant pair at 2.70 kHz with Q 1.24, to within 0.02 dB rms. Together with the integrator that is close to a fourth-order Butterworth low-pass with −3 dB at about 2.84 kHz. The filter's resonance offsets the integrator's early droop, so the passband is flat.

The DAC: some errors are VIDC's, some the chip's, and the mismatch is the board's

  • The ± halves match on the A3010: −0.01 to −0.08 dB, against the A3020's −0.54 dB. The mismatch comes from the board, most likely the tolerances of the hybrid's subtractor resistors, not from VIDC.
  • Shared by both chips, so part of VIDC's design: chords 1 to 3 have steps 11–20% too large, and the level falls back at codes 48 and 64 (A3010: −0.34 and −0.53 i; A3020: −0.40 and −0.33 i).
  • Particular to each chip: the A3010 falls by 5.1 i at code 112, where the top chord starts (the datasheet expects a rise of 4 i), and its chord 6 steps are 13% large. The A3020 has neither. Overall the two curves differ by 0.72 steps rms.

Noise with the filter in

−75.7 dB below a full-scale sine, at the recorder's own floor, with the line whine at −117 dB. The filter takes out what little high-frequency noise the bypassed machine had; the bypass costs about 3 dB of noise, which is still 35 dB below the DAC's conversion error.

A second A3020: how much boards vary

Tests 1 and 2 on a second A3020 with the filter bypassed ("A3020b"), at line-in level 60. Files: recordings/*-a3020b-no-filter-level-60.*.

  • The integrator varies by about 7%. A3020b's fits as a first-order low-pass at 3.43 kHz (time constant 46.4 µs), against 3.20 kHz (49.7 µs) on the first A3020: component tolerance. Treble is −9.6 dB at 10 kHz, against −9.8 dB. A treble boost designed for 3.3 kHz suits both to within about 0.5 dB.
  • The ± mismatch differs again. A3020b's halves match to within 0.03 dB through chords 1 to 4, but the negative half is 0.17 to 0.38 dB low in chords 5 to 7. That is a level-dependent compression of the negative half, not the first A3020's even 0.54 dB, and the A3010 has almost none. Each board has its own.
  • The VIDC design errors repeat on a third chip. Chords 1 to 3 have steps 9–19% too large, chord 4's are 13% small, and the level falls back at codes 48 and 64 (−0.23 and −0.82 i). At code 112 it falls by 1.5 i: between the first A3020 (+3.9, as the datasheet expects) and the A3010 (−5.1). That step is where chips differ most.

Back to the A3020: noise

Noise is not a problem

Silence measures −73 dB below a full-scale sine, and the recorder on its own (the Arc switched off) −76.6 dB. That puts the Arc's own noise at about −76 dB, mostly a little hum below 2 kHz: 100 Hz at −87 dB. The 15.625 kHz line whine is at −98 dB, inaudible. +0 and −0 are the same, and MODEs 12 and 13 are the same. That is 35 to 40 dB below the DAC's own conversion error, so noise needs no work. With sound DMA switched off it is slightly louder, −71 dB, with tones at 274 and 374 Hz; QTM never runs like that.

4 channels, one slot playing a 100 Hz square of the code shown against 0. Each value is the level in dB relative to 48 µs with the other slots at zero. With the other slots at zero, every rate down to 12 µs per frame (3 µs slots) holds within 0.3 dB: the DAC settles in time even at the fastest rate. With the other three slots at full scale, positive steps read about 0.5 dB high and the negative step up to 1.8 dB low, so a loud channel changes the gain of the others, which in music means intermodulation between channels. The effect shrinks as the rate goes up, from −1.8 dB at 48 µs to −0.4 dB at 12 µs: another reason to run fast. This phase also captured the carrier itself: with the slots at different levels, a steady 20.8 kHz tone at 48 µs, which at 32 µs and faster moves above what the recorder can hear.

Stereo: exact positions, 37 dB separation

The centre and the two-thirds positions measure as the datasheet says: left shares of 0.502, 0.665 and 0.339. At the hard positions 1.5% leaks into the other side, a separation of 36.7 dB. Sound_Stereo values of ±85 still gave the hard positions, so the 83% and 17% positions need a value between 42 and 85; they weren't measured.

Candidate improvements

Ranked by expected gain against effort. All but the last cost nothing at runtime.

  1. Undo the 3.2 kHz roll-off. Measured on two bypassed A3020s: a first-order roll-off at 3.20 and 3.43 kHz, 10 dB down at 10 kHz. A first-order treble boost undoes it exactly. This can be done after the Arc, on the board with a smaller integrator capacitor, or in the samples during the prep pass, aimed at the pitch each sample is mostly played at. measured
  2. Run at 24 µs or faster on a bypassed machine. Without the filter, 48 µs puts images as loud as the tones into the audible band. This doubles the mixer's load at 4 channels on an ARM2. model
  3. Store samples at 2× their rate, band-limited, in the offline prep pass. About 6 dB less resampling distortion at any sample period, for twice the memory. tools/prep and the gg variant already do offline sample preparation. model
  4. Replace QTM's conversion table. By default with the datasheet-nearest table, which beats the OS table on both machines at every level: about 2 dB more SINAD at full scale and up to 3.6 dB at −12 dBFS. Optionally with a table measured on the user's own machine, which gains up to 7 dB at −6 dBFS and cuts THD by up to 16 dB, but only on that machine. measured
  5. Build the volume table from the same curve. This fixes the 0.2 to 0.7 dB gain error and gives all 65 volumes their own level where the codes allow it. model
  6. Use sources with more than 8 bits for new music. Converted straight to log, they hold about 35 dB SINAD down to −42 dBFS, against 10 dB for 8-bit. model
  7. Try noise-shaped conversion. Choose log codes by error diffusion, so the conversion error moves to high frequencies. Not yet modelled.
  8. Analogue clean-up is not needed for noise. Test 3 put the Arc's noise at about −76 dB and the line whine at −98 dB. measured
